This page records each request by Asheville residents for police oversight since 1991, what was delivered, and the state-law limits on what a city board may do. As of Sept 8, 2026, no civilian police oversight body in Asheville is meeting.

## The record, crisis by crisis

**1991 to 2020: the Citizens Police Advisory Committee.** Asheville's Citizens Police Advisory Committee was created in 1991 as a "liaison": no investigative power, no records access, no discipline role, members removable by council "with or without cause." In 2018 the city considered dissolving it; it last met in March 2020. Never abolished, never convened; the city's own page says "not currently meeting." A CPAC member told council in 2020: "I don't think CPAC needs to exist anymore." ([City page](https://www.ashevillenc.gov/department/city-clerk/boards-and-commissions/citizens-police-advisory-committee/) · [WLOS](https://wlos.com/news/local/asheville-looks-at-forming-citizen-oversight-board-for-police-discipline-issue))

**2017–2019: the 2017 beating of a pedestrian by an APD officer.** An APD officer beat, choked, and tased a Black man walking home from a 13-hour shift. The public learned of it only because **someone illegally leaked the bodycam video**: NC's bodycam law (G.S. 132-1.4A) had kept it sealed through six months of internal handling in which the officer was allowed to resign before the video became public. The reforms that followed: a Human Relations Commission **given no investigative power**; a written-consent-to-search policy that council ordered in May 2018 and APD implemented in **October 2019, seventeen months later**, after the chief refused, and the Police Benevolent Association **threatened legal action against the five council members who voted for it**. The officer's felony guilty plea ended in probation, dismissal, and expungement. ([ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/news/criminal-law-reform/beaten-tased-and-arrested-jaywalking-while-black) · [Mountain Xpress on the PBA threat](https://mountainx.com/news/police-group-threatens-legal-action-against-asheville-city-council-on-recent-policy-changes/) · [city announcement, Oct 2019](https://www.ashevillenc.gov/news/asheville-police-implements-written-consent-to-search/))

**2020.** After the tear gas and the medic station, Chief Zack was "truly sorry"; [[Esther Manheimer|Mayor Manheimer]] called it "senseless... wrong." City Attorney Brad Branham promised a new kind of board with "much deeper access to records... a much more formal role in the process of reviewing disciplinary situations", pending state authorization. What was delivered: a **~3% budget reallocation** (animal control and noise moved out of APD); an **internal** after-action review, ten months later, that found the tear gas "within agency policy"; **no publicly confirmed discipline for any officer** (not for the gas, not for the medic station), with 500+ tear-gas complaints filed and every outcome sealed by state personnel law. And the promised board? That was [HB 399](https://www.ncleg.gov/BillLookUp/2021/H399), sponsored by the *entire Buncombe legislative delegation*, pre-negotiated to be modest (no subpoena power, no public findings, use-of-force cases only). **It died in Raleigh without a hearing.** Meanwhile the city spent six figures on a California PR firm billed as "transparency engagement advisers." ([Mountain Xpress on the after-action](https://mountainx.com/news/apd-releases-after-action-report-on-summer-protests/) · [WLOS on the promised board](https://wlos.com/news/local/asheville-looks-at-forming-citizen-oversight-board-for-police-discipline-issue) · [[Camera Network Misuse|Camera network misuse, documented cases]])

**2026: the Public Safety Committee.** The council committee assigned RTIC oversight, chaired by [[Bo Hess]], had requested no audit and received no post-approval briefing through its July 29, 2026 meeting ([[The Committee|meeting-by-meeting record]]). On Nov 20, 2025 eight callers asked it to cancel the Flock and Fusus contracts; staff answered that no data goes to federal agencies, and no motion followed. It next meets <span data-cal="asheville-public-safety"></span>; the audit reports required by G.S. 20-183.31(a)(7) are the record to ask for.

**2026: the RTIC.** Council approved a citywide surveillance apparatus with a resolution stating it would be used "solely for lawful purposes." The resolution **designates no oversight body, creates no reporting duty, and contains no enforcement mechanism**, and it was passed in a city where, at that moment, no civilian oversight body was meeting: CPAC dormant six years, the Human Relations Commission conducting no business since Helene, the Reparations Commission **formally dissolved** in October 2025 with its criminal-justice recommendations punted to a future budget. None of the four recovery boards created in 2025 has a policing role. A resident, a contract negotiator by profession, said it at the podium: "We aren't protected by civil liberties statements." ([BPR](https://www.bpr.org/politics-government/2026-05-13/asheville-council-accepts-police-tech-funding-hears-budget-proposal) · [BPR on the boards](https://www.bpr.org/bpr-news/2025-07-14/asheville-weighs-changes-to-citizen-advisory-boards) · [Urban News on the commission's dissolution](https://theurbannews.com/government/2025/city-of-asheville-dissolves-reparations-commission/))

## What state law allows a city board to do

The statutes:

- **[G.S. 160A-168](https://www.ncleg.gov/enactedlegislation/statutes/html/bysection/chapter_160a/gs_160a-168.html)** makes personnel files confidential. A city official who showed a review board an officer's disciplinary file without a court order would be **committing a misdemeanor**. Boards cannot see the records they'd need to review anything.
- **No NC city may grant its board subpoena power.** Only the General Assembly can, and bills to allow it ([2015](https://reason.com/2015/03/27/nc-police-reform-bill-would-permit-civil/), [2017](https://www.ncleg.gov/BillLookUp/2017/H165), Asheville's own 2021 local bill) have **all died without hearings**.
- The boards that exist under these limits have these records: **Charlotte's** review board heard 102 appeals in ~25 years and sided with complainants **twice**, and both times the chief rejected the finding. **Winston-Salem's** considered 11 cases in 9 years and sided with police **every time**. **Durham's** has granted 2 hearings since 2011. Dawn Blagrove of Emancipate NC, on the record: "These boards are doing exactly what they are designed to do by system actors, which is **create the facade of accountability without creating actual accountability**." ([WFAE](https://www.wfae.org/2022-05-11/ahead-of-todays-hearing-a-look-at-the-history-of-charlottes-citizens-review-board) · [Winston-Salem Journal](https://journalnow.com/news/local/government-politics/winston-salem-police-review-board-has-only-considered-11-cases-in-last-9-years-it/article_e7679c5a-6635-11ef-b7f8-473700eed084.html) · [ABC11](https://abc11.com/citizen-review-board-police-accountability-brutality-raleigh/6305390/))

## What this record means for the RTIC

Put the three records together:

1. **[[Camera Network Misuse|Camera network misuse, documented cases]]** records the misuse cases elsewhere; the safeguards did not catch them before an audit or a records request did.
2. **This page** shows Asheville cannot build real oversight even when it wants to: state law withholds the powers a review board would need, and bills to grant them have not been heard.
3. **The [[Promise Tracker]]** records the city refusing to release its Flock audit logs in September 2025; the Axon plate-reader portal now publishes retention and device counts, and no audit result has been published.
